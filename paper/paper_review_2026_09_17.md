Paper review, 17 September 2026

**What was reviewed and changed.** I compared the research diary with the code, saved training settings and histories, and tables of results at repository revision `02073d0`, the identifier for that saved version of the project. The paper now has an updated title and abstract, a dated review of methods and conclusions, and explanations where earlier interpretations need qualification. The sequence of experiments and recorded measurements are preserved. Training code and saved experiments were unchanged. The accompanying advisor overview gives the shorter meeting narrative; the saved review evidence records the calculations behind the findings below.

**Overall assessment.** The diary explains why the project moved from one experiment to the next. Its main readability problem was that old conclusions, proposed experiments, and completed work could all sound current. The abstract also stopped before the latest ZBL and fine-tuning results. ZBL is a formula that adds strong repulsion between very close atoms; fine-tuning means continuing to adjust an already trained model. The dated review now explains where the project stands while retaining the earlier reasoning as a record of progress. Some historical claims about physical correctness and statistical certainty need the qualifications below.

**The most important finding concerns which data count as unseen.** A training set supplies examples used to adjust a model. A validation set helps choose settings and a checkpoint, which is a saved version of the model. A test set should remain separate for the final assessment. A seed makes random choices repeatable, including the choice of examples in each set. The main training-and-evaluation script uses matching seeds for training and testing individual snapshots. The rollout script, which runs the model forward to produce a trajectory, independently uses seed 42 to choose starting snapshots.

For the documented ordering of 1,593 WaterBox configurations, the six rollout starts have dataset indices 234, 1446, 23, 1133, 514, and 1083. An index is simply an example's position in the dataset. All six belong to the training sets of the models trained with seeds 0 and 1. Calling them “held out,” meaning unseen during training, was therefore incorrect for those models. The heating and comparisons from matching starting points remain informative. They show behavior from familiar starting configurations, with performance from unseen starting configurations still to be assessed.

**Fine-tuning also changes the data divisions.** By default, fine-tuning creates a new split using seed 42. Its available training pool includes 129 examples that were in the original seed-1 model's test set. Those examples could be drawn for further training; the audit does not establish how many were actually drawn. The reference sample used to define desired simulation properties also comes from the full dataset. Under default settings, it includes 16 seed-42 test examples and 23 original seed-1 test examples. These counts refer to different test-set definitions and can overlap.

The rules for detecting unstable simulations use all 160 seed-42 test configurations. Specifically, an “instability floor” is a minimum acceptable distance between atoms in different molecules; distances below that threshold trigger a rewind during training. Choosing these thresholds from test data allows that data to influence training. This is a form of data leakage, which weakens the independence of a later test. Some original run commands were not saved, so changes to default seeds and the examples used in before/after comparisons still need checking. Repeatedly using test results to guide method development also makes those results part of development. Future confirmation needs a separately reserved evaluation set.

**The physical meaning of the added penalty needs care.** Net force is the sum of the forces on a molecule's atoms; torque measures their tendency to turn the molecule. In a liquid, neighboring molecules can legitimately cause both acceleration and rotation. A loss term that pushes each molecule's net force and torque toward zero therefore imposes an additional preference. Such a term is called a regularizer: it steers learning toward behavior we hope will be useful. Its usefulness must be judged against physical accuracy and simulation results.

For an isolated system, the relevant conservation properties follow when the predicted energy stays unchanged under a translation or rotation and forces are derived from that energy. This is energy invariance. Equivariance describes how vector predictions rotate with the input; invoking equivariance alone left part of the argument unstated. The paper now clarifies this distinction. It also notes that adding squared force and squared torque requires attention to units, because they measure different things. The underlying momentum balance is described in [TU Delft's mechanics text](https://interactivetextbooks.tudelft.nl/nb1140/content/momentum.html).

**Several reported measurements need clearer definitions.** The value labelled maximum “per-molecule” penalty is calculated by first averaging over molecules in each snapshot, then taking the largest snapshot average. A particularly bad individual molecule can be diluted by that first average. The torque calculation also needs a check at periodic boundaries. A periodic box connects opposite faces; one molecule can appear split across those faces in its stored coordinates. “Unwrapping” places its atoms together in a consistent image of the box before using their positions to calculate quantities such as torque. The current calculation does not explicitly do this.

The training loss uses mean squared error, or MSE: square prediction errors and average them. Reported accuracy often uses mean absolute error, or MAE: average the sizes of errors without squaring. These measures emphasize large errors differently. Energy and force loss weights multiply errors with different units and scales, so weights of 0.05 and 0.95 do not imply that 5% and 95% of learning come from the two terms. WaterBox explicitly converts energies to electronvolts, or eV, and distances to ångströms, or Å, where 1 Å is 10⁻¹⁰ metres. Aspirin's eV labels still require verification: the project has no explicit MD17 conversion, and the [upstream loader](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torchmd/torchmd-net/main/torchmdnet/datasets/md17.py) copies raw energy and force values. The installed software and cached dataset on the training machine determine the actual units.

**The latest fine-tuning result leaves uncertainty.** A paired comparison uses the same starting positions and velocity choice before and after fine-tuning, helping separate the model change from differences in initial conditions. Across five pairs, the average temperature difference is +3.57 K. A conventional paired t calculation, which estimates uncertainty from the spread of those differences, gives an approximate 95% confidence interval of −42.17 to +49.30 K. It assumes independent velocity draws and approximately normally distributed differences. With only five pairs from one model and one starting geometry, the interval is broad. The result has not resolved an improvement, and both cooling and heating remain compatible with the data under these assumptions.

The reported increase in energy error also needs a check that the original and fine-tuned models were evaluated on exactly the same examples. In addition, the comparison between global and local observables changed the snapshot cap—the maximum number of simulated configurations processed for one update. An observable is a measurable property, such as an average bond length or the distribution of distances between atoms. Because more than one setting changed and the fine-tuning experiments were not repeated with independent seeds, we cannot assign the observed difference solely to the choice of observable.

**Our procedure differs from published StABlE.** [The published method](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13984v3) searches for unstable regions and repeatedly trains from nearby starting states. The project instead uses fixed simulation windows, rewinds a simulation when it fails the distance check, collects accepted snapshots, and makes one model update per phase. Each replica—a separate simulation—continues from its current state across updates. These choices make the implementation an adaptation whose behavior needs its own validation.

Sampling also needs checking. Equilibrium means the simulation has settled into the intended statistical distribution, even though individual atoms keep moving. Newly generated snapshots are not automatically equilibrium samples. Nearby snapshots, and molecules within the same box, can share information. Thus roughly 512 molecular entries from eight snapshots do not necessarily provide the information of 512 independent observations. “Effective sample size” describes how many independent observations a correlated sample is roughly equivalent to; it has not been measured here. We also need to check that reference data and simulations describe compatible temperatures, densities, and treatment of nuclear motion. This is what matching the reference ensemble means. Keeping only snapshots that pass the stability check may further affect the sampled distribution. The approximation used to assign energy to each molecule and the balance between the two training objectives also need checking.

**Some statistical conclusions were too strong.** Many molecule-level measurements come from the same configuration, and successive training iterations share history. Treating all of them as independent can make uncertainty appear smaller than it is. Estimates should account for these groups and time correlations. Examining several replicas and then highlighting one that improved also increases the chance of finding an apparently interesting pattern by chance. This is the multiple-comparison issue. Repeating velocity draws measures one model's sensitivity to starting motion; separately trained models are needed to assess training variability. The association between force error and heating across six configurations is a useful lead, but remains a small-sample observation. The direction and size of paired differences should be considered together with their uncertainty.

**Training and evaluation conventions are now documented together.** The paper records data counts, architecture, units, batch sizes, accumulation, loss weights, learning-rate settings, checkpoint selection, ZBL settings, and simulation choices. A batch is a group of training examples; accumulation combines several small batches before an update to reduce memory requirements. An epoch is a pass through the training set. Annealing here means changing the energy/force weights at a chosen epoch. A learning-rate scheduler changes the size of parameter updates during training. The [upstream implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torchmd/torchmd-net/main/torchmdnet/module.py) uses AdamW, an optimizer that adapts updates to each parameter, and a scheduler that responds to validation loss. The exact version on the training machine still needs recording. Making checkpoint scores comparable before and after annealing does not automatically make the scheduler's decisions comparable too.

The best saved scores match history labels 68/68 for seed 0 and 63/66 for seed 1, in control/regularized order. These labels were previously described too broadly as identical. Logging happens at particular points in the training loop, so the actual checkpoint files should confirm the precise epochs. The results column named `final_epoch` gives the last history row, which can differ from the epoch of the selected best model. For simulations, “plateau temperature” means the average over the last 30% of recorded frames. Energy drift means the change in total energy from the first to the last frame, divided by atom count. Temperature can rise while total energy stays nearly constant if potential energy is converted into kinetic energy.

**Information still needed for repeatability.** Save the training machine's software versions, GPU model and numerical precision, original commands, exact dataset membership, and complete fine-tuning settings. Preserve random-generator states when exact continuation is needed. File hashes—fingerprints of file contents—can identify the precise dataset and model files used. These details belong in a run manifest, a saved record of how each experiment was performed. Current fine-tuning histories lack a complete manifest, and actual checkpoint files are unavailable locally. The exact MD17 count and units, the mix of physical conditions in the reference data, sample correlations, and comparisons with published numerical results also need checking. This review examined selected sources rather than every citation in the paper.

**The next useful comparison.** First preserve consistent training, validation, and test sets and verify the fine-tuning assumptions. Then compare ordinary continued supervised training with training that also uses simulation observables, giving both the same update budget. The ordinary continued-training case is the control: it helps show how much change comes simply from more training. Evaluate both on identical untouched examples and repeat with several independently trained models. Measure energy/force accuracy, heating, molecular structure, and how long a simulation runs before a predefined failure. Current trajectories span about 1 picosecond, or 10⁻¹² seconds, so longer runs are needed to establish usefulness over longer simulation times.
