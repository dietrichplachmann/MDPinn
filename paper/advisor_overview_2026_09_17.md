Advisor overview, 17 September 2026

**The main problem.** We want a learned molecular potential that reproduces reference energies and forces and supports physically useful molecular dynamics. The project began by asking whether an auxiliary physics-based loss helps supervised TensorNet training. The experiments expanded into a second question: why can a model perform reasonably on static configurations yet produce rapid heating when used to simulate trajectories?

**The models being compared.** All completed model comparisons use the same TensorNet architecture. The interventions are ordinary supervised energy/force training, an added molecular net-force/net-torque penalty, a short-range ZBL repulsive prior, and a version of that prior excluding atom pairs within the same water molecule. The latest experiments fine-tune an existing supervised TensorNet checkpoint using simulated observables plus an energy/force regularizer. MACE and NequIP provide literature context; they have not been trained as project baselines.

**Why the system changed.** Initial MD17 aspirin tests found the whole-system momentum penalty almost redundant. Energy-derived forces already obey the relevant whole-system identities when the scalar energy has the required invariances. We moved to periodic water, where each molecule interacts with its surroundings. The added molecular penalty supplies a training signal, but individual water molecules physically exchange momentum. Its interpretation is an empirical regularizer, and its numerical value is not a measure of broken momentum conservation.

**The core training design.** WaterBox contains 1,593 DFT-labelled configurations, each with 64 waters and 192 atoms. Each seeded random split has 1,274 training, 159 validation, and 160 test configurations. TensorNet has six layers, 256-dimensional embeddings, 64 radial basis functions, a 5 Å cutoff, and neighbor limit 128. Initial learning rate is 10⁻⁴. Energy/force MSE coefficients begin at 0.05/0.95 and switch to 0.75/0.25 at epoch 30 in annealed runs; the molecular penalty coefficient is 0.01. The nominal effective batch is 32. The latest supervised runs allow up to 70 epochs and restrict best-checkpoint eligibility to epochs 50–69. Different training seeds also change the data partition, so their variation includes both training and split effects.

**What the trajectory work established.** Static errors did not adequately describe the observed rollout heating. We investigated short-range contacts, force decomposition, ZBL, integration timestep, and checkpoint selection. The stock ZBL configuration introduced further difficulties. With molecular exclusions and revised checkpoint selection, the regularized models had lower mean temperatures across two training seeds and two rollout designs. Both conditions still heated substantially. This remains a small, exploratory comparison; several earlier findings changed after additional seeds or methodological corrections.

**What we are trying now.** The current approach is StABlE-inspired fine-tuning. It alternates Langevin sampling and observable-based parameter updates while retaining supervised energy/force information. We tested global radial distribution functions and local mean O–H bond lengths. The latest local run used eight replicas, 20 updates, a learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁶, and a 0.1 fs integration step. The available five-pair NVE comparison gives about 768 K before and 772 K after fine-tuning. The mean paired difference is +3.6 K with an approximate 95% interval of −42 to +49 K, conditional on one checkpoint and starting geometry. It has not demonstrated an improvement. This implementation differs from the published StABlE phase/reset procedure, so the result is specific to the adaptation tested here.

**The rigor issue identified in this review.** Rollout selection uses split seed 42, while the compared checkpoints were trained with split seeds 0 or 1. All six rollout starting configurations are in the training sets of both checkpoints. Fine-tuning also reconstructs its own split, samples observable targets from the full dataset, and calibrates instability thresholds on its test partition. These findings limit claims about held-out generalization and require a consistent split manifest before confirmatory comparisons. Existing trajectory behavior remains useful diagnostic evidence.

**The decision for discussion.** The next defensible comparison needs fixed, preserved data partitions and a verified fine-tuning protocol, followed by a supervised-only fine-tuning control and an observable-based condition on the same untouched evaluation configurations. The scientific decision is whether to retain the molecular force/torque penalty as an empirical intervention, focus on observable-based stability training, or first strengthen the supervised baseline. Success should mean improved trajectory behavior together with acceptable reference accuracy, replicated across independently trained models.
