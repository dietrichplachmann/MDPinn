Advisor overview, 17 September 2026

**The main problem.** We want a model that predicts how atoms interact accurately enough to run useful molecular simulations. A molecular potential predicts the energy of an arrangement of atoms; the way that energy changes as atoms move gives the forces acting on them. Molecular dynamics, or MD, uses those forces to move atoms forward in small time steps. Our starting question was whether adding physical rules during training improves these models. The work then raised another question: why can a model predict individual examples reasonably well yet make a simulated system heat up rapidly?

**What we have compared.** All completed comparisons use TensorNet, the neural-network design that learns the relationship between atomic positions and energy. We first trained it on reference energies and forces, a process called supervised learning. We then added a penalty for each molecule's total force and turning force, or torque. Another change was ZBL, a standard formula that strongly pushes atoms apart when they get too close. We also tested a version of ZBL that leaves out pairs of atoms within the same water molecule. These are different versions of the same underlying model. MACE and NequIP provide literature context; our project has not trained them for direct comparison.

**Why we moved from aspirin to water.** In the initial aspirin experiments, the extra whole-molecule penalty was nearly zero already: the model's energy-based construction and symmetries supplied the relevant conservation properties. Water offered many interacting molecules in a periodic box. “Periodic” means opposite box faces connect, approximating a small part of a larger liquid. There is an important qualification: a water molecule can legitimately accelerate or rotate because neighboring molecules push on it. The molecular force/torque penalty therefore adds a preference whose benefit must be tested experimentally. Its size should not be interpreted as an amount of broken momentum conservation.

**The data and how we divide it.** WaterBox contains 1,593 configurations, meaning snapshots of atomic positions, each with 64 water molecules and 192 atoms. Their reference energies and forces come from density functional theory, or DFT, a quantum-mechanical calculation. We divide them into 1,274 training examples for adjusting the model, 159 validation examples for monitoring training and choosing a saved model, and 160 test examples intended for a final assessment on unseen data. A random seed is a number that makes a random choice repeatable. In the current setup, changing this seed changes both the training randomness and which examples enter each group.

**The main training settings.** TensorNet uses six processing layers, 256 learned features to represent each atom, and 64 functions that describe interatomic distances. Its interaction cutoff is 5 Å, where an ångström is 10⁻¹⁰ metres, with a neighbor limit of 128. The initial learning rate, which controls the size of parameter updates, is 10⁻⁴, or 0.0001. Each update normally combines information from 32 configurations; small batches are accumulated to fit GPU memory.

Training minimizes a loss, a numerical measure of prediction error. The energy and force terms use mean squared error, or MSE, which squares errors before averaging and gives large errors more influence. Their weights start at 0.05 and 0.95 and change to 0.75 and 0.25 at epoch 30. An epoch is one pass through the training set; this scheduled weight change shifts attention toward energy accuracy. The weights are multipliers, so their values do not directly state each term's share of learning. The molecular penalty has weight 0.01. Recent runs allow up to 70 epochs. The best checkpoint—a saved set of model parameters—is chosen only from epoch indices 50–69, the final 20 possible epochs. Epoch numbering starts at zero.

**What the simulations taught us.** Errors measured on individual snapshots did not adequately predict the heating seen in rollouts, meaning trajectories generated by repeatedly applying the model's forces. We investigated close atomic contacts, different components of the forces, the time step, and how saved models were selected. After excluding same-molecule pairs from ZBL and changing checkpoint selection, models with the extra force/torque penalty had lower average temperatures across two training seeds and two ways of varying starting conditions. Both versions still heated substantially. These are preliminary findings from a small comparison, and earlier conclusions changed as we improved the checks.

**What we are trying now.** We are testing fine-tuning: continuing to adjust a previously trained model. The approach is inspired by StABlE, a published method that learns from properties measured in the model's own simulations. We tried radial distribution functions, which describe how often atom pairs occur at different distances, and the average O–H bond length within a water molecule. During training, Langevin dynamics adds random forces and friction to maintain a target temperature. Several separate simulations, called replicas, supply examples. The model also continues learning from reference energies and forces to discourage loss of its earlier accuracy.

**The latest result.** The local bond-length run used eight replicas, 20 updates, a learning rate of 0.000003, and a time step of 0.1 femtoseconds; a femtosecond is 10⁻¹⁵ seconds. We then compared the original and fine-tuned models using five matching initial-velocity choices in NVE simulations, which keep particle number and volume fixed, have no thermostat, and aim to conserve total energy. Average temperatures were about 768 K before and 772 K after. The paired increase was 3.6 K, with an approximate 95% confidence interval from −42 to +49 K. This interval expresses uncertainty in the average difference under the statistical assumptions: the small comparison remains compatible with either some cooling or some heating. It has not demonstrated an improvement. Our procedure also differs from published StABlE, limiting how broadly we can interpret this result.

**The review finding we need to address.** The rollout script chooses its “test” examples with seed 42, while the compared models were trained using seeds 0 or 1. All six rollout starting configurations were actually training examples for both models. Fine-tuning also uses test configurations when setting its rules for detecting instability. This allows evaluation data to influence development, often called data leakage. The observed heating remains useful evidence about model behavior, but assessing performance on unseen examples requires consistent, preserved data divisions.

**The decision for discussion.** We should first establish trustworthy data divisions and check the fine-tuning procedure. Then we can compare ordinary continued training with training that also uses simulation properties, giving both the same update budget and untouched evaluation examples. The broader choice is whether to keep exploring the molecular force/torque penalty, focus on simulation-based fine-tuning, or improve the ordinary supervised model first. Success should include better simulated behavior and acceptable reference accuracy, repeated with several independently trained models.
